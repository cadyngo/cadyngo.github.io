##### **Skin in the Game: Electrodermal Arousal, Expectations, and Bubbles in Experimental Asset Markets**

(with Thomas Henning, Ross Spoon, Filip-Mihai Toma, Colin Camerer, and Alec Smith)

<label for="abstract-bubbles" class="abstract-toggle-label">Abstract</label> [Poster](/assets/pdf/Ngo_Cady_Poster_Bubbles.pdf) [Slides](/assets/pdf/Ngo_Cady_Slides_Bubbles.pdf) [OSF](https://osf.io/rg4hb/overview) [Paper](mailto:chngo@caltech.edu)<sup class="upon-request-mark">*</sup>

<div class="abstract-text" markdown="1">
We examine the extent to which price forecasts, emotional arousal, and risk preferences predict the formation and crash of price bubbles in experimental asset markets with a constant fundamental value (CFV). Bubbles are common even with higher stakes and repeated-session experience. Both linear regressions and flexible machine learning models identify lagged returns and forecasts as the strongest predictors of next-period asset returns. Biometric measures of arousal, from individual electrodermal activity (EDA), add little predictive power once lagged returns are controlled for. However, EDA predicts personal trading behavior and performance: high earners exhibit anticipatory arousal before a market crash, whereas low earners display reactionary arousal after the crash begins. Elicited price forecasts fail rationality tests but are well-explained by some adaptive models. Finally, individual risk preferences show no association with market returns, suggesting bubble formation is driven primarily by belief dynamics rather than risk tolerance.
</div>

##### **Feeding Attendance: The Impact of California’s Universal Meals Program on Public School Absenteeism**

<label for="abstract-feeding" class="abstract-toggle-label">Abstract</label> [Poster](/assets/pdf/Ngo_Cady_Poster_UMP.pdf) [Slides](/assets/pdf/Cady_Ngo_MEA_2025.pdf) [Paper](mailto:chngo@caltech.edu)<sup class="upon-request-mark">*</sup>

<div class="abstract-text" markdown="1">
This paper investigates the effects of California's Universal Meals Program (UMP), first implemented in the 2022-2023 academic year, on chronic absenteeism in public K-12 schools. By providing free meals to all students, regardless of income, the UMP aims to address food insecurity and improve student outcomes. Given that absenteeism is a strong predictor of academic disengagement and future criminal behavior, this research examines whether universal meal access can reduce absentee rates, particularly in high-need districts. Using a difference-in-differences approach, the study compares absenteeism trends before and after the UMP's implementation, accounting for variations in treatment intensity across districts categorized by poverty level. Preliminary expectations suggest that districts with higher proportions of students previously eligible for free or reduced-price meals will see more significant improvements in attendance. The findings could have important implications for educational policy and crime prevention strategies, providing evidence for the long-term benefits of universal school meal programs.
</div>

##### **How Curiosity Overcomes Fear**

(with Bo Zhang, Emily Towner, and Dean Mobbs)

<label for="abstract-curiosity" class="abstract-toggle-label">Abstract</label> [Github](https://github.com/Curiosity-Fear-Decision-Making/Curiosity-Fear-Decision-Making-Task)

<div class="abstract-text" markdown="1">
Curiosity is an intense, rewarding, and wondrous state that can impel us to risk life and limb to satisfy it. Across three studies, we show that people are remarkably willing to expose themselves to danger to satisfy their curiosity. First, participants were willing to incur financial costs to learn the answer to a clickbait statement, and experienced regret when they chose not to pursue it. Second, we extended this finding to physical risk: participants were willing to place their hand into a box despite varying probabilities of encountering a tarantula in order to obtain the answer to a clickbait question. Third, using functional neuroimaging (fMRI), we demonstrate that the drive to approach danger and the suppression of threat are supported by distinct neural systems—activity in the striatum was associated with the drive to approach danger, whereas orbitofrontal cortex activity was associated with suppressing the perceived risk of electric shock in the service of satisfying curiosity. Together, these results reveal a competition between curiosity and threat-avoidance drives, such that when curiosity is sufficiently strong, people are willing to take real risks to satisfy their need to know.
</div>
